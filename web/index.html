First principles

The room is a matrix: n needs by m characteristics, each cell 9, 3, 1 or blank. The order of the rows and the columns carries no information. Shuffle them and every weight, rank and roof pair stays the same; what changes is what the eye can see. Diagonalising means choosing a row order and a column order so that each need sits beside the characteristics that chiefly serve it, and the relationships gather along the line from the top-left corner to the bottom-right. Three things then show that the listed order hides:

  • Blocks. Groups of needs served by the same group of characteristics, with little outside the group: the natural modules of the design, and candidate owners.
  • Couplings. Relationships that no ordering brings to the diagonal: a characteristic that serves needs in several blocks, or a need that depends on several blocks. These are where one team's change moves another team's result.
  • Shape. Axiomatic Design (Suh 1990) reads a design matrix by its shape. Diagonal is uncoupled: each need has its own characteristic. Triangular is decoupled: there is an order in which to settle the characteristics so that each need is met once and not revisited. Anything else is coupled, and the design iterates.

The method, step by step

  1. Positions. Row i gets pi = its position ÷ (n − 1) and column j gets qj = its position ÷ (m − 1), so the rectangular matrix has a diagonal from corner to corner. The cost of an order is J = Σ rij (pi − qj)², the weighted squared distance of the relationships from that diagonal.
  2. Spectral order. Minimising J over all orders is NP-hard (it is seriation). Let the positions be real numbers instead and the minimiser is the second eigenvector of the Laplacian of the bipartite graph that has a node per need, a node per characteristic and an edge of weight rij between them: the Fiedler vector (Atkins, Boman and Hendrickson 1998), equivalently the second singular vectors of the degree-normalised matrix (Dhillon 2001). Sorting rows and columns by their coordinates gives the spectral order. Parts of the house that share no relationship are ordered separately, largest first; empty rows and columns go last.
  3. Local search. From the spectral order, move one row or one column at a time to the position that lowers J most, until no move helps; the same idea as the bond-energy algorithm (McCormick, Schweitzer and White 1972) and King's rank-order clustering (1980), with J as the score.
  4. Blocks. Cut the ordered rows and columns into k diagonal blocks to maximise bipartite modularity (Barber 2007): the mass inside the blocks beyond what the row and column totals would put there by chance. Dynamic programming makes the cut exact for the order; k is the number of blocks with the highest Q unless you fix it above.
  5. Coupling. Count the relationships that fall outside their block. None: uncoupled. All on one side of the diagonal: decoupled, and the side says which blocks to settle first. Both sides: coupled, and the smaller side is the set of ties to design away or to accept as iteration (Benavides and Rodríguez 2011, after Suh's own reorganisation algorithm). The ● level and the full 9/3/1 level are read separately, because QFD practice treats the strong relationships as the structure and the weaker ones as influence.

How to read it, and the caveats

  • A block is a candidate module and a candidate owner: its needs are met by its characteristics. Whether the blocks follow your team structure, or cut across it, is itself a finding.
  • A characteristic with much of its weight outside its block is platform-level: change it and several blocks move. The priority rank alone does not show this.
  • A need spread across blocks has no single home. Ask whether it is really two needs, or whether a characteristic is missing.
  • A perfect diagonal is not the goal of a QFD room. If every need had exactly one characteristic, the characteristics would be the needs restated in units and the house would have found nothing. Diagonalising is for seeing structure, not for scoring it.
  • The order is a mathematical result of the ratings. Change a rating and the order can change: this view recomputes live, while the House keeps the order you applied until you apply it again.
  • A roof trade-off (− or −−) inside a block is a design decision for that block's owner; across blocks it is a negotiation between owners.
  • For square matrices, Rinderle's reangularity and semangularity (1982) score coupling as angles between the columns; they do not apply to a rectangular room, so the measures here are the share of weight inside the blocks and the mean distance from the diagonal.

What it is

A House of Quality (the first QFD matrix) links what the stakeholders need (WHATs, the rows) to the measurable characteristics of the design (HOWs, the columns), and shows which characteristics matter most. The method, step by step, is in docs/method.md; every formula is in docs/formulas.md.

Left wall
n needs, each with its source and an importance from 1 to 5.
Ceiling
m characteristics with a unit and a direction of improvement.
Room
How strongly each characteristic drives each need.
Roof
How the characteristics help or fight each other. Each diamond sits where the diagonals from two columns meet.
Right wall
Today's score against the goal per need, giving an improvement ratio and an adjusted weight.
Basement
Target, today's state, weighted importance, relative share, must-pass gate, rank, difficulty and the links per characteristic.
Diagonal
The same room with its rows and columns reordered so the relationships gather along the diagonal: the modules of the design, and the couplings between them.
Daily
The screen for every day: what blocks go-live (the gates and their items), what to do next (the critical path), where the tracker and your tests disagree, what is overdue, and every work item with its status, your verdict (Verified / Partial / Fails / Not built) and the date you last tested it. Planning holds the House, the Diagonal and the lists for the weekly read.

Scoring

Importance
5 essential, 4 very important, 3 important, 2 nice to have, 1 minor. Set with the stakeholders; record who set it and when.
Must-pass gate
A characteristic that must pass before anything ships, whatever its weight. It keeps its weighted importance in the room but is never ranked against the feature characteristics.
●9
Strong: this characteristic decides whether the need is met.
○3
Medium: clear influence.
△1
Weak: some influence.
++
Strong positive: improving one strongly helps the other.
+
Positive: helps.
−
Trade-off: improving one tends to hurt the other.
−−
Strong trade-off.
↑ ↓ ◎
Larger is better, smaller is better, meet a set target.
Weighted importance
Per column: Σ over needs of (weight × relationship). Weight is the importance, or importance × (goal ÷ today) when "Importance × gap" is on.
Today / goal
1 poor to 5 excellent. Ratio = goal ÷ today; adjusted weight = importance × ratio. "Today" is one named moment, the build or prototype the scores describe, set on the Input tab ("The today scores describe"); its name replaces the word "today" in the headers.

How to edit

  • Click a room cell to cycle blank → △ 1 → ○ 3 → ● 9 → blank. With the keyboard: 1, 3, 9 set a value, 0 or Delete clears, arrows move.
  • Click a dot in the Importance column to set 1 to 5.
  • Type in the two small boxes on the right wall for today's score and the goal.
  • Click a roof diamond to cycle none → ++ → + → − → −−, or use the Roof pairs view.
  • Click the dashed difficulty box to step 1 to 5.
  • The lists themselves (needs, characteristics, work items, title) are edited on the Input tab, where you also load and save files.
  • Order: Diagonal reorders the house itself into the blocks found in the Diagonal view (thick lines mark the block edges); As listed puts it back.
  • Rows and columns: With relationships only hides the needs nothing serves and the characteristics that drive nothing, so a big house reads smaller. The weights, ranks and shares do not change.
  • Download SVG draws the house as a picture, in the order and reduction shown; the same drawing comes from the command line and the MCP server. Download Markdown (Input tab) writes the whole house as a report an agent or a person can read.
  • An agent connected to the MCP server proposes room cells and roof pairs; they show as ? in the House and wait on the Input tab until you confirm or reject them. Every value you set on the page is recorded with your name (the author field) in the file's provenance.
  • Copy as TSV puts the whole house on the clipboard for Excel, with the export date and the scoring mode in its first row: send the dated TSV around as the version of record. Page edits stay in this browser.
  • Daily / Planning switches between the screen for every day and the weekly QFD read. Words: Auto shows plain words on Daily (need, spec, go-live blocker) and the QFD words on Planning (WHAT, HOW, must-pass gate); pick one to fix it. Every export (TSV, CSV, Markdown, SVG) keeps the QFD words whatever the setting.
  • Statuses: drop a tracker export (a Jira CSV with an "Issue key" column) on Daily or on the Input tab. Statuses, due dates, points and the tracker's update time are merged; your verdicts, retest dates and notes are kept; the day the export was read becomes the day "overdue" is judged against.
